# Story architecture

Use for a novel, season, multi-chapter arc or major restructuring. The goal is an evolving chain of choices, not a sequence of canon cameos.

## Establish the engine

Name the protagonist's concrete pursuit, the relationship or belief that complicates it, and the cost of leaving things unchanged. An external task and an internal conflict should interfere with each other. A healer trying to reopen a ward might need institutional permission from someone whose family they harmed during the war.

Set the ending condition early enough to plant its causes. It may be an unresolved but transformed relationship, a solved mystery, an institution changed in a specific way, or a decision the protagonist could not make at the start. Avoid assuming every story needs a war-scale climax.

## Arc layers

Keep only the layers the project uses:
- External objective: changing obstacles and available choices.
- Relationship: trust, disagreement, reciprocity and repair.
- Knowledge: what is learned, misread, hidden and disclosed.
- World consequence: rules, labour, institutions and material costs.
- Motif: an object, ritual or place whose meaning develops.

Link these through scenes. If an investigation advances but nobody's options or relationships change, the chapter may feel informational rather than dramatic. A meal can carry a decisive political refusal; a duel can leave the real interpersonal question untouched.

## Chapter architecture

For each planned chapter, record a short causal unit in the existing outline: starting situation, competing wants, choice under pressure, consequence, and the question carried forward. Vary endings: a discovery, a commitment, an emotional reversal, an arrival, or quiet recognition. A compulsory cliffhanger can destroy rhythm.

Use the academic year, holidays, exams and changing weather when they affect time pressure or access. Do not reproduce the original series' school-year pattern merely because the setting is Hogwarts. Adult work, travel and family obligations can organise the calendar instead.

## Ensemble and antagonist plans

Give significant secondary characters independent calendars. They may solve a problem offstage, misunderstand a warning or make a demand at an inconvenient time. Define what the antagonist can actually observe and resource. A hidden adversary should not become omniscient whenever the plot needs a setback.

Alternate perspectives only when they add necessary action or understanding. Track what the reader learns ahead of the focal character and how that changes suspense. Repeating the same scene from several viewpoints needs a distinct payoff.

## Stress test the outline

Remove one central character: does the solution or emotional conclusion change? Remove one magical limitation: does the plot collapse because the limitation was invented at the last minute? Delay a revelation: which scenes become impossible? Check that the final choice grows out of earlier practice, failure and relationships.

Book dossiers offer comparative craft interpretations. Use them to diagnose mechanisms—restricted information, institutional denial, aftermath—not to transplant the sequence of scenes.

Deliver the scale requested: a compact arc proposal, full chapter outline or revised structure. Mark open decisions and alternatives. Do not write a complete parallel planning system when the project already has one.
