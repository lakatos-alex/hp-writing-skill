# Mystery and revelation design

Use when a story depends on uncertain identity, hidden motive, missing history, a magical mechanism or a delayed explanation.

## Model facts and explanations separately

A clue is an observable fact; a theory is an interpretation. For every decisive clue, record its location, who encounters it, plausible initial reading, true significance and eventual reinterpretation. The reader should be able to recognise the earlier fact after the reveal, even if they misunderstood it.

PoA's central confrontation revises betrayal history; HBP's memory investigation depends on incomplete and altered evidence. These are different mechanisms. Choose what your investigation needs rather than accumulating every kind of mystery.

## Give evidence an information route

A witness must have seen, heard, received or inferred the thing they report. Record how much they know and why they speak now. Secrecy can be caused by shame, coercion, mistaken confidence, conflicting obligations or lack of access. “They never thought to mention it” becomes weak when the information would obviously change several earlier decisions.

For magical evidence, ask what the tool shows and what it cannot show. A Map name is not a motive. A memory is not an unedited legal record by definition. A portrait's access to a room does not supply every living person's history. A truth potion cannot automatically cure errors in the speaker's beliefs.

## Fair misdirection

A misleading clue should make sense in both readings. Give an innocent person a real secret or a separate reason for suspicious behaviour; do not have the narrator conceal something the focal character is plainly thinking solely to fool the reader.

Red herrings need consequences. An accusation may damage trust or cost time even after it is corrected. Keep the wrong theory proportionate to what the characters could reasonably know. Intelligence does not require instant certainty, and error does not require sudden stupidity.

## Revelation as a scene

The reveal changes someone’s next action. Place it under pressure: a witness may leave, an ally may refuse the cost, a misunderstood object may already be in use. Avoid resolving the entire plot through an uninterrupted explanation if the affected people have conflicting objectives.

After revelation, trace which earlier choices remain blameworthy and which become understandable. New context can complicate responsibility without cancelling it. Allow people to disagree about the ethical interpretation after the factual mechanism is settled.

## Audit

Read the planned ending against each setup. Check clue visibility, chronology, access, alternate explanations and payoff. Then run the knowledge-route check in the continuity workflow if a delayed disclosure affects many scenes. A simple mystery may need only a short note; large cases can use a table in the existing outline.
