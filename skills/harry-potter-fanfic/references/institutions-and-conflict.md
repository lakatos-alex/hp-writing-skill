# Institutions, prejudice, and nonhuman societies
Use alongside the locations reference when a plot involves governance, work, resistance or competing rights.

## The Ministry changes with time
**Before the return is acknowledged:** GoF36 shows denial despite testimony; OotP8 and OotP15 show legal and educational pressure. A department can act against evidence because admitting it threatens leadership.
**Open war:** HBP1 and HBP16 show the political need for visible action and endorsement. Security policy can be ineffective or coercive without every employee supporting Voldemort.
**Capture:** DH8 and DH12–13 show takeover followed by routine administration and persecution. Bureaucracy can turn ideology into interviews, paperwork and enforced classifications.
**Writer's consequence:** date the scene before selecting a law or officeholder. Do not give a first-year Ministry the assumptions of the captured wartime Ministry.

## Hogwarts is education and government
Houses, prefects, staff appointments, governors, detentions, examinations and decrees distribute authority. OotP15–18 and OotP29 show rules being contested through teaching and collective action.
A house membership is social context, not a moral diagnosis. Slytherin is not a species of villain, Ravenclaw does not confer omniscience, Hufflepuff is not weak, Gryffindor is not always right.
Ancient Runes is an academic subject in the books; a comprehensive spell-programming or energy-grid system is fanfic invention. Distinguish a plausible extension of study from an attested magical technology.

## Blood status, class, and belonging
CoS7 makes slurs and their harm visible in peer conflict; DH13 makes persecution bureaucratic. Muggle-born, half-blood and pure-blood labels express ideology, not a scale of talent.
Weasley poverty and Malfoy wealth show different options; wealth does not automatically imply Ministry office or hereditary legislative seats. Treat noble titles, marriage contracts, heir tests and magical inheritance ceremonies as AU material unless sourced.
Muggle-born characters can retain expertise and family ties without becoming universal experts in modern technology or outside politics.

## House-elves: differing perspectives under coercion
**CoS18:** Dobby's liberation requires action and changes his circumstances.
**GoF8–9 and GoF21:** Winky and Dobby respond differently to dismissal, freedom and employment. Hermione identifies exploitation, but her proposed solutions are not universally welcomed.
**DH10:** Kreacher's understanding of Regulus and his treatment affect his behaviour.
**DH23–24:** Dobby's rescue is a chosen and costly act.
**Scene rule:** name the elf, their objective, their knowledge and the practical terms of any cooperation. Avoid presenting all elves as a unified political voice. A cheerful worker is not sufficient evidence that a coercive institution is just; nor should their own expressed wishes be discarded because a protagonist has a reform plan.

## Goblins and ownership
DH24–26 puts Griphook's expertise, the sword and competing understandings of ownership at the centre of a bargain. Do not reduce the disagreement to one party mysteriously being untrustworthy.
Ask what each side thinks payment buys, what has been promised, what remains concealed and what would count as betrayal to each participant. The viewpoint character's normal property assumptions are not neutral facts.

## Werewolves and social exclusion
PoA18 presents the condition, schooling risk and Wolfsbane; OotP29 and DH11 show the connection between stigma, employment and fear about family. A condition can create practical risk without making a person morally monstrous.
Wolfsbane access, housing, disclosure and employment are concrete problems. A romance cannot cure the condition; acceptance also requires choices about resources, privacy and safety.

## Giants, centaurs and other communities
GoF24 and OotP20 connect Hagrid's family history and diplomacy to prejudice and danger. OotP27 and OotP33 expose centaur politics and the costs of treating them instrumentally.
Do not assign every member of a species the same motive. Hospitality, territorial rights, war alignment and old grievances can differ. The protagonists may misunderstand a refusal because they assume their crisis takes precedence.

## Press, reputation and resistance
Rita Skeeter's reporting in GoF18, GoF24 and GoF28 links access to distortion and reputational injury. OotP25–26 provides an alternative publicity route; OotP16–19 shows organisation through teaching.
Ask who controls distribution, who risks identification, who is persuaded and who benefits from disbelief. A public correction rarely repairs private humiliation instantly.
Resistance requires logistics: a meeting place, transport, communication, supplies, secrecy and decisions about trust. Use these as opportunities for character disagreement, not just technical setup.
